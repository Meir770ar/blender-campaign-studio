# Director operating system for premium campaign films

Read this before treatment, storyboard or shot creation. The goal is not to maximize visual activity. It is to control what the viewer notices, understands, feels and remembers. “Cinematic,” “epic,” “premium” and “viral” are outcomes to demonstrate, not executable direction.

## Decision order

Resolve each beat in this order:

1. **Outcome:** what should change in the viewer or in the demonstrated state?
2. **Evidence:** what visible or audible fact earns that change?
3. **Point of view:** whose experience or knowledge organizes the moment?
4. **Action and blocking:** what changes inside the frame?
5. **Frame:** what is included, excluded and prioritized?
6. **Light and color:** what becomes legible, hidden, attractive or uneasy?
7. **Camera:** does it stay, follow, reveal, approach or withdraw, and why?
8. **Cut and sound:** what motivates the transition, and what carries across it?

Do not decide on an effect, lens number or camera move before the earlier questions. A valid direction can be quiet, static, imperfect or dark when that is the clearest expression of the beat.

## Story architecture for short campaigns

Build a causal progression rather than a list of attractive shots:

- **Entry condition:** what the audience sees or believes at first contact.
- **Hook:** a precise contradiction, question, action, image or sound that opens a gap. It must be truthful and paid off.
- **Orientation:** enough context to understand the subject without stalling the film.
- **Development:** escalating evidence, action or emotional pressure. Each beat adds something new.
- **Proof:** the demonstration, human reaction, detail, fact or transformation that earns the claim.
- **Resolution:** the new state after the proof.
- **Action:** a specific next step whose wording, destination and factual details are verified.

For a 6–20 second spot, several functions may share one shot. Do not force every label into a separate segment. For an interview-led film, performance and meaning determine timing; do not crush every breath into a formula.

## Directing the viewer's attention

For every shot specify a focal path: where the eye begins, where it moves, and where it lands at the cut. Use the smallest number of simultaneous signals needed.

Available signals include:

- relative size, position and negative space;
- contrast, saturation, brightness and sharpness;
- leading lines, frames within frames, shapes and pattern breaks;
- foreground/middleground/background relationships;
- face, gaze, gesture and object movement;
- focus change, light change, sound cue and camera movement.

If four cues compete, the frame has no hierarchy. If an important label or expression loses to a highlight, texture or background motion, redesign the frame rather than adding another cue.

## Composition as meaning

- **Positive and negative space:** allocate importance and emotional distance. Leave room in the direction of gaze or motion unless pressure, entrapment or imbalance is intended.
- **Depth:** foreground, middle and background should establish relationship, scale or reveal—not exist only as decoration. Deep focus can hold competing information; shallow focus can isolate or withhold it.
- **Balance:** symmetry suggests control, formality or ritual; asymmetry can create movement or unease. Neither is automatically premium.
- **Lines and shapes:** use them to point, contain, divide or connect. A product silhouette must remain readable against the dominant background shape.
- **Color and tone:** the eye tends toward contrast and brightness. Protect the intended focal point from brighter irrelevant elements.
- **Aspect:** recompose for each output. A crop is not a vertical direction. Define focal position, safe areas and background behavior per aspect.

Composition guides are planning aids, not pass/fail rules. Rule of thirds, symmetry, golden guides and centered packshots are alternatives selected for the beat.

## Shot size, angle and lens intent

Describe the perceptual reason before the technical value:

- wide for geography, relationship, scale, isolation or blocking;
- medium for action and interpersonal distance;
- close detail for proof, texture, decision or intimacy;
- high angle for overview or reduced agency; low angle for presence or threat; level for directness;
- wider perspective to exaggerate depth and motion; longer perspective to compress layers and detach observation.

A focal length in an AI prompt is an intent, not calibrated optics. In Blender it is measurable, but sensor fit, camera distance and subject scale still determine the image. Avoid changing lens and position only to “make it cinematic”; protect perspective continuity when adjacent shots share a space.

## Camera movement grammar

Start with a static camera. Add movement only when it does a named job:

- **follow:** maintain relationship with moving action;
- **reveal:** change what the audience knows;
- **emphasize:** push toward a decision, feature or proof;
- **detach/contextualize:** pull away to expose scale, isolation or result;
- **transfer attention:** pan, tilt, rack focus or handoff between subjects;
- **reorient:** cross space or the action line in a readable motivated move;
- **embody:** convey a controlled subjective state through instability or inertia;
- **transition:** create a designed visual or motion bridge to the next shot.

Static is an active choice. When the camera is fixed, blocking, lighting, focus, production design, performance and cut duration must develop the shot internally.

For every movement define start composition, end composition, focal path, velocity profile, settling behavior and cut handle. Avoid default orbits, constant-speed slides and automatic push-ins. Camera and object motion should not cancel each other accidentally.

## Blocking and staging

Blocking is the visible expression of intention, relationship and change. Specify:

- subject/object starting positions and orientation;
- target or obstacle;
- action verb and intention, not a facial label alone;
- eyelines and screen direction;
- entrances, exits, handoffs and depth-plane changes;
- hand/product contact and any state that must match the next shot;
- the exact visual event that motivates the cut.

Use movement inside the frame before moving the camera. A handoff can pass attention by gaze, gesture, motion, focus or sound. In a product film, blocking includes the order in which body, feature, label, material and result become visible.

## Screen geography and continuity

Establish an action line for interacting subjects or directional movement. Staying on one side preserves screen direction and eyelines. Crossing is allowed when the shot itself carries the viewer across, a neutral/on-axis image resets geography, or disorientation is intentionally part of the beat.

Maintain or deliberately change:

- identity, wardrobe, product geometry, label and color;
- location, time, weather and motivated sources;
- object positions, fill level, damage and hand contact;
- direction of travel, gaze and light;
- movement velocity and phase at match cuts;
- emotional/performance state and spoken meaning.

Record a start and end state per shot plus an explicit continuity link to the next. “Looks similar” is not a continuity contract.

## Coverage and selectivity

Coverage exists to preserve meaning and editing options, not to collect every angle. For each scene decide whether one designed shot communicates it more clearly than many cuts. Add establishing, action, reaction, insert or detail only when it provides unique information, emotion, proof or a continuity bridge.

For paid/generated motion, approve the coverage plan before submission. Prefer one well-designed action plus useful handles to several vague alternatives. Create a second paid variation only for a named creative question within the approved attempt budget.

## Product and brand direction

- Confirm the exact product geometry, label, logo, color and claims before stylization.
- Decide the hero feature and reveal order. Do not illuminate every surface equally.
- Use edge highlights and reflections to describe material and form; protect label readability separately where necessary.
- Give scale cues when size matters. A floating product without contact, shadow or environmental response may feel synthetic.
- Choreograph mechanisms, liquid, particles and exploded parts around the product; the effect must not obscure the proof or mutate the brand asset.
- End on a stable, readable brand state long enough for the delivery context. Do not rely on a one-frame logo or a CTA covered by platform UI.

## Performance and voice direction

Direct an actor or narrator with circumstance, intention and action: whom they address, what they want, what changes and what they try to do. Avoid asking for a generic “warm,” “excited” or “premium” voice without playable context.

For interviews, preserve meaning and human timing. Select takes for thought clarity, presence, truthful emotion, pronunciation and continuity. Do not splice words into a claim the speaker did not make. For synthetic narration, audition a short representative passage, lock pronunciation and voice, then synthesize coherent paragraphs within authorization.

## Editorial and sound direction

Every cut needs a motive: new information, emotional change, action, gaze, sound, graphic relation, shape, color or rhythm. Cut on musical beats only when the beat supports meaning. Let an action or sound begin before/after the picture when a J/L cut improves continuity or anticipation.

Design sound in perspective:

- dialogue/narration carries meaning;
- ambience establishes place and scale;
- foley gives material contact and weight;
- designed movement/impact supports a specific visual event;
- music controls expectation, energy and resolution;
- silence creates contrast and attention.

A whoosh on every transition erases hierarchy. Reserve strong sonic events for strong story events.

## Director gates

### Before styleframes

- outcome, audience, single message, proof and action are known;
- point of view and emotional/knowledge change are explicit;
- visual and sonic systems plus anti-rules are defined;
- claims and brand assets have sources.

### Before paid motion or simulation

- shot purpose, visible action, start/end state and focal path are concrete;
- styleframe/reference is approved and content-hashed;
- blocking, camera motivation, lighting motivation and continuity are resolved;
- acceptance and rejection criteria are observable;
- exact provider/model/attempt limit or simulation cost is authorized.

### Rough-cut review

- watch once for story without stopping;
- watch once silently when autoplay matters;
- review performance/meaning, repetition, cut motivation and ending;
- record timestamped defects before adding decorative polish.

### Final review

- narrative and proof;
- identity, brand and continuity;
- motion, physics and AI artifacts through the whole clip;
- Hebrew, numbers, CTA and safe areas per aspect;
- color, exposure and product/skin integrity;
- dialogue, pronunciation, mix, sync and delivery encode.

Critical factual, rights, identity, missing-media, RTL, pronunciation or continuity failures block delivery. A subjective alternative should be presented as a choice, not mislabeled as an error.

## Anti-patterns

- moving the camera because a static shot “looks basic”;
- three-point lighting as a mandatory recipe;
- adding bloom, particles, speed ramps or camera shake without a beat-level purpose;
- using a high-contrast look to hide weak exposure or material definition;
- cutting every musical beat mechanically;
- filling every empty area instead of preserving hierarchy;
- calling a single start/middle/end inspection a full motion review;
- treating an automated score or successful render as evidence of premium direction.
